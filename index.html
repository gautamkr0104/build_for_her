📜 Passage 1 — The Library That Floats
On the Ganges at Patna, a blue-and-white boat drops anchor every morning near a sandy stretch of the riverbank. It is no ordinary ferry. Shelves line both sides of its cabin, and about fifteen hundred books — Hindi and English storybooks, science titles, biographies and comics — sit ready for young readers. The floating library began when a group of teachers noticed that children in the riverside slums and in families who live on boats hardly ever touched a book. Schools were far away, and the city's libraries were beyond their reach. So the district administration and a team of volunteers fitted out an old country boat with shelves, mats and a small solar-powered fan. Every day the boat stops at four or five points along the river. Children wade through the shallow water or climb in from their own boats, choose a book and read under the awning while the librarian marks their cards. A book may be kept for a week; there is no fee, only a promise to return it. In two years the boat has enrolled over two thousand young readers. Teachers at nearby schools say that regular borrowers now read faster and write more confidently in class. The boat costs little to run, but its real cargo, the volunteers say, is not books at all — it is the habit of reading.
📜 Passage 2 — The Last Passenger
Bhagalpur was waking up when Ramu Kaka pedalled his rickshaw out of the lane. The first train had just come in, and he hoped for a good fare before the sun grew harsh. His last passenger that morning was an old woman in a white sari who got down near the post office in a great hurry. As Ramu turned the rickshaw around, he spotted something glittering on the seat — a thin gold chain. He knew at once it was hers. Without a second thought, he pedalled back towards the post office. The old woman was still there, searching the ground near the gate, her spectacles on the tip of her nose and her hands trembling. "Maaji, is this yours?" Ramu asked, holding out the chain. She burst into tears and blessed him again and again, saying the chain had been her late husband's only gift to her. She pressed a five-hundred-rupee note into his hand. Ramu returned it gently. "Keep your blessing, Maaji," he said. "That is worth more." Word of the quiet rickshaw puller spread through the neighbourhood that week, and the old woman's son, a schoolteacher, put Ramu's story on the blackboard for his class to write about. Ramu himself remained unchanged. He still rises before the first train and still returns whatever his passengers leave behind — an umbrella, a lunchbox, a schoolbag, once even a sleeping puppy.
